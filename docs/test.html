THE CENTRAL PUZZLE

Research scope

Resource wealth should accelerate development, yet most resource-rich emerging markets remain trapped in low-complexity exports. We ask why.

Primary Questions

  • How successful have emerging market economies been at developing value-added industries from natural resource extraction?
  • What factors explain the divergence across countries?

Secondary Questions

  • How sustainable are these shifts in terms of fiscal revenues, employment and export diversification?
  • How have they supported or hindered credit quality?
  • What policy levers and institutional conditions support or hinder the transition?
FROM DETERMINISM TO CONDITIONAL MECHANISMS

The resource curse is conditional

The debate has shifted from whether resources harm growth to identifying the conditions under which they do.

Mehlum, Moene & Torvik (2006)

Institutions determine outcomes

Resources impede growth only where institutions are "grabber-friendly". Where rule of law is strong, resources accelerate growth.

Channel: Institutional quality mediates the resource-growth relationship

Venables (2016)

Consumption crowds out investment

Resource-rich governments consistently fail to convert extraction rents into human capital, infrastructure, and financial assets. Adjusted net savings are strongly negative.

Channel: Intertemporal allocation failure

Van der Ploeg & Poelhekke (2009)

Volatility is the indirect curse

Resource abundance boosts growth directly but undermines it through macroeconomic volatility, shifting investment toward short-term, low-return projects.

Channel: Price volatility → fiscal instability → underinvestment

Isham et al. (2005)

Resource type matters

Point-source resources (oil, diamonds) concentrate rents among elites. Diffuse resources (agriculture, forestry) spread benefits more broadly.

Channel: Rent concentration → elite capture → institutional erosion

Our approach: We test Venables' investment hypothesis using Mehlum et al.'s interaction methodology, while ML techniques explore which structural predictors emerge beyond the theory-driven specification.

MEASURING PRODUCTIVE SOPHISTICATION

The Economic Complexity Index

The ECI, developed by the Harvard Growth Lab, captures the depth of productive "know-how" accumulated by a country. It measures the transition from low-complexity activities (subsistence farming, crude oil extraction) toward high-complexity activities (electronics, machinery, chemicals).

How it works: Based on trade data using two properties: Diversity (how many products a country exports competitively) and Ubiquity (how many countries export those same products). A country that exports many rare, complex products scores high.

TESTING VENABLES' INVESTMENT HYPOTHESIS

Econometric results

Model Specification

$$\begin{aligned} ECI_{it} = \beta_0 &+ \beta_1 HCI_{it} + \beta_2 GFCF_{it} + \beta_3 Prod_{it} \\ &+ \beta_4 HCI \times Prod + \beta_5 GFCF \times Prod \\ &+ \text{Controls} + \beta_{12} ECI_{i,t-1} + \epsilon_{it} \end{aligned}$$

Panel OLS with country-clustered standard errors. Controls include political stability, rule of law, trade openness, and forestry rents.

Key Findings

Human Capital (HCI) is positive and significant across all specifications. The coefficient falls once lagged ECI is included (1.64 to 0.29) but remains significant, indicating HCI contributes to complexity growth beyond existing trajectory.

Gross Fixed Capital Formation is positive and significant throughout.

Production Value is negative but not significant, consistent with the conditional curse view.

Institutional controls lose significance once lagged ECI is included.

HUMAN CAPITAL RETURNS ARE AMPLIFIED BY RESOURCE INTENSITY

The interaction effect

The interaction term HCI x Production is positive across all specifications. The association between human capital and complexity is stronger in countries with more intensive resource production, consistent with Venables (2016): investment returns are greatest where the gap between current and optimal capital stocks is widest.

WHEN ALL THREE PILLARS COLLAPSE

Republic of Congo

🇨🇬

The Triple Deficit

Oil: 40% GDP, 80% exports ECI rank: 124th Cluster: Oil Exporters

Congo illustrates what the model predicts: when all three pillars fail, ECI collapses regardless of oil wealth. Pro-cyclical fiscal spending means education and infrastructure budgets rise and fall with the oil price.

  • 50% electricity access (12% rural); distribution losses over 50%
  • Only 13% of roads paved; rail system below capacity
  • 75% of workers in informal employment
  • Education spending oscillated between 8.4% and 1.6% of GDP
  • Only 18% of adults hold a formal account

Peer Comparison

Angola shares a similar export structure but reached higher income levels via larger production volumes and Chinese-backed infrastructure programmes.

Azerbaijan expanded produced capital by 428% since 1995; Congo managed only 40%.

Malaysia reinvested oil revenues into technical education and vocational training, generating broader spillovers.

GDP GREW. COMPLEXITY COLLAPSED.

Azerbaijan

🇦🇿

Dutch Disease in Action

Oil & Gas: 90% exports, 50% GDP ECI rank: 95th Cluster: Oil Exporters

Azerbaijan's ECI declined 0.68 points from 1995 to 2019 while GDP growth averaged 6.6% annually. The sharpest ECI deterioration (2000 to 2008) coincided with 20% annual GDP growth, a textbook Dutch Disease signal.

  • Infrastructure spending concentrated on pipelines and Baku
  • Rail freight halved between 2006 and 2016
  • NPLs reached 21% after the 2015-16 banking crisis
  • Over 45% of "non-oil" manufacturing is petroleum-related
  • SOFAZ spending at times exceeded its own investment returns

The Malaysia Counterfactual

Both countries were upper-middle-income hydrocarbon economies in the mid-1990s with comparable ECI levels. Malaysia channelled Petronas revenues into downstream manufacturing and electronics, reaching ECI +1.1 by 2019. Azerbaijan collapsed to -1.2 by the mid-2000s.

Two binding constraints: severely inadequate rule of law (-0.7 vs Malaysia's +0.5) and extreme hydrocarbon concentration crowding out non-oil tradables.

STRONG INSTITUTIONS ARE NECESSARY BUT NOT SUFFICIENT

Chile

🇨🇱

The Human Capital Bottleneck

Copper: 57% exports ECI rank: ~70th (declining) Credit rating: A2/A/A-

Chile has the strongest institutions in Latin America, a structural fiscal balance rule, and two sovereign wealth funds. Yet its ECI has declined since the mid-2000s. The binding constraint is not governance but human capital quality and R&D capacity.

  • R&D spending: 0.34% of GDP (one-sixth of OECD average)
  • PISA maths scores 60 points below OECD average
  • Worker productivity 50% below OECD average
  • Exports cluster in the "periphery" of the product space
  • 54% of copper exports go to China for downstream processing

Supply Chain & Peer Comparison

363 producing facilities across 17 commodity groups generate ~$62bn in annual mineral value. Production is concentrated in three northern regions (85% of output).

Botswana mirrors Chile's fiscal discipline but also fails to diversify, confirming that sound institutions alone do not produce complexity gains.

Norway shows the counterfactual: universities and research institutes integrated into the oil industry, producing engineering firms that later diversified into offshore wind and subsea technology.

THREE LEVERS FOR THE NEXT DECADE

Conclusions and implications

The most effective pathway is not to abandon resource dependence but to systematically convert resource rents into the structural foundations for long-term productive diversification.

1. Infrastructure is key

Unreliable energy supply suppresses private investment. Electricity access is the most consistent infrastructure predictor of complexity across all models.

2. Financial Depth

Domestic credit to the private sector emerged as the single strongest ML predictor, yet is absent from most theory-driven specifications. Monitor it as a filter for diversification capacity.

3. Human Capital

Returns to education are highest where resource extraction is most intensive. Investment must be counter-cyclical and structurally insulated from commodity prices.

For sovereign credit assessment: The constraints on industrial upgrading shift as institutional and economic conditions improve. In low-income resource economies (Congo), all three pillars are binding simultaneously. In middle-income settings (Azerbaijan), Dutch Disease and governance dominate. In upper-middle-income settings (Chile), human capital quality and R&D become the bottleneck. No single policy lever is sufficient across all settings.
